01

MBCS

Multi-Bay Car Service Centre · My Best Work
View full case study ↗
Role
UI/UX Designer, sole designer
Duration
2-week sprint
Platform
Enterprise SaaS, desktop
Industry
Automotive after-sales
Team
Solo, working with stakeholders, workshop managers and AI-assisted prototyping
Tools
Figma, ChatGPT, Google AI Studio, Claude, GitHub. AI assisted; every design decision was mine.
Status

Problem

Many cars, limited bays, whiteboards and manual job cards. The manager couldn't see which car was delayed, which bay was blocked or what job was holding it.

What I did

Interviewed workshop managers, then mapped the manager's journey and one car's journey across manager, advisor, mechanic and parts team. Then IA, flows, wireframes, prototype and the live floor view.

Design decisions

  1. Reframed the brief: flow, not job trackingWhyManagers kept describing the floor, not the jobs. A job marked "in progress" says nothing about whether work is moving, where it is stuck or what it will cost.RejectedThe job-list screens I designed in week one.
  2. Detect and explain, and the human actsWhyA late part is normal; what hurts is how long a blocked bay stays invisible. The system flags the blockage and its cost to the pickup promise, and the manager moves the car to a Holding Area.RejectedAutomated detection (drifts into the alerts managers rejected), recommendation approval (rubber-stamping) and root-cause investigation (too heavy to do standing up).
  3. A three-second live floor view, not a dashboardWhyDashboards report the past. One manager walked his floor every 90 minutes because no screen showed it live. Every bay, the queue and the holding area are visible at once, and colour is used only for status.RejectedA dashboard of cards, charts and a jobs table.

Trade-offs

  • More information vs simple screen: chose simple. Only standing-mode actions stay on the board; invoices, records and history moved out.
  • Flexibility vs control: chose manager control. The system flags, the manager decides, and every move is reversible.
  • Real-time updates vs noise: colour only for status, so the board stays quiet until something needs attention.

Edge cases

  • Customer not answering approval call
  • Part not arriving today
  • Job bigger than expected
  • Sudden pile-up of cars

Design targets

The targets the board was built to hit, from the case study. These are targets, not measured results.

Blocked-bay time42 min → 12 min
On-time pickup74% → 95%
Spotting a bottleneck15 min → under 1 min
Allocating a bay3 min → 30 sec

Outcome & learning

Moved the thinking from tracking cars to managing the complete service operation. The problem is not always one bad screen; sometimes it's the whole system.

02

Goibibo

Flight Booking · A Difficult Problem
View full case study ↗
Role
Senior UX Designer
Timeline
8 weeks
Platform
Goibibo mobile app
Industry
Travel and tourism
Research
Usability test with 3 users (2 scenarios each) and competitor analysis
Status

Problem

Too much information made it hard to see the difference between fares and what you actually get for the price.

What I did

Observed users, tested with 3 participants and reviewed competitor apps, then focused on the fare comparison in the booking journey: flow, information structure, comparison experience and final UI.

Design decisions

  1. A compact sticky flight contextWhyUsers kept scrolling back to check they were still on the same flight. The problem was missing confidence, not missing information.RefinedMy first plan, a full sticky flight card, risked distracting from fare comparison, so it became a compact one.
  2. Side-by-side fare comparisonWhyLong vertical cards turned comparison into a memory test: users forgot one fare while reading the next. A horizontal layout shows all fares and key attributes at once.RejectedLong vertical fare cards.
  3. Expand the selected fare into one detail cardWhyUsers kept opening "Show more" because they did not trust what they had seen. Baggage, cancellation, date change and price now sit in one card.RejectedKeeping details collapsed (clean, but high effort) and showing them on tap (flexible, but repeated actions).

Trade-offs

  • Information vs simplicity: progressive disclosure, but the selected fare's key details show immediately instead of hiding behind "Show more".
  • Business info vs user understanding: replaced a vague "Recommended" label, which users found manipulative, with context-aware guidance such as "best value for flexibility".
  • Speed vs detailed comparison: comparison is opt-in, so fast bookers keep a quick path and analytical users can open "Compare all fares".

Edge cases

  • Different fare benefits
  • Price changes mid-journey
  • Baggage and add-ons differ
  • Going back without losing context

Usability test results

Measured in the case study's usability test with 3 participants and 2 scenarios each. Treat as directional evidence, not a statistically significant result.

Fare decisions42% faster
"Show more" taps65% fewer
Flight confusion0%

Outcome & learning

Clearer fare comparison with less effort before booking. Good UX is showing the right information at the right time.
